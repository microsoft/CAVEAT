# 1 Introduction

Computer-use agents (CUAs) operate web browsers to complete tasks on a user's behalf \citep{openai2025operator, anthropic2024computeruse, browseruse2024}, and purchasing is one of their first large-scale applications: commercial assistants now support direct checkout with merchants \citep{openai2025instantcheckout, google2026ucp}, and industry forecasts project that agents will mediate 10 to 20% of U.S. e-commerce by 2030 \citep{morganstanley2025agentic}. In a delegated purchase, the user specifies preferences in natural language, and the agent searches a marketplace, compares alternatives, and completes the transaction. Whether the purchased product satisfies the stated preferences therefore depends on search and comparison decisions that the user does not observe.

Online marketplaces, however, are optimized to influence these decisions. Platforms and sellers tune ranking, badges, urgency cues, and defaults to increase conversion: an audit of more than eleven thousand shopping websites identified 1,818 instances of dark patterns, with higher prevalence on more popular sites \citep{mathur2019dark}, and regulatory actions against manipulative interface design include a 2025 FTC settlement with a major e-commerce platform over its subscription interfaces \citep{ftc2025subscription}. At the same time, much of the influence buyers encounter is neither deceptive nor directed at AI agents: sponsored placement, earned badges, and scarcity labels can be entirely truthful and still change which products a buyer examines and selects. We refer to such marketplaces as naturally adversarial: their influence arises from ordinary commercial incentives rather than from attacks on any particular agent, and it predates the deployment of CUAs.

Existing evaluations do not measure agent behavior under this pressure. Capability benchmarks, from WebShop and Mind2Web to WebArena, OSWorld, and high-fidelity replicas of commercial sites \citep{yao2022webshop, deng2023mind2web, zhou2024webarena, koh2024visualwebarena, xie2024osworld, real2025}, use neutral storefronts, as do shopping-specific suites \citep{peeters2025webmall, deepshop2025}. Security evaluations study deliberate attacks on agents, such as prompt injection, adversarial pop-ups, and hidden instructions in page content \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo, zhang2024popups, liao2024eia, ma2024caution, evtimov2025wasp}; their threat model assumes an adversary who targets the agent, whereas marketplace steering targets buyers in general and arises from ordinary commercial practice. Closest to our work, recent studies inject dark patterns into websites and find that agents often engage with them \citep{guo2025susbench, ersoy2025darkpatterns, decepticon2025, tang2025gui}, and simulations of agent markets report susceptibility to manipulation \citep{bansal2025magentic, allouah2026agent}. These studies measure whether an agent interacts with a manipulative element. They do not measure what that interaction costs the user, because the cost is realized in the final purchase, and none of these settings defines which purchase is correct. Measuring this cost requires environments with a verifiable optimal purchase, matched clean and steered conditions, and transaction-level evaluation.

We introduce CAVEAT, a benchmark for CUAs in naturally adversarial marketplaces. CAVEAT contains nine environments modeled on widely used commercial platforms, spanning general retail, grocery and food delivery, resale and handmade goods, freelance services, and short-term lodging. Each is a functional storefront with search, product pages, cart, and checkout, populated by a catalog generation pipeline. Tasks are issued by synthetic users whose instructions encode seven preferences. Preferences are either absolute constraints (e.g., a budget cap) or comparative objectives (e.g., the cheapest option satisfying all constraints). Because steering operates by changing how alternatives are surfaced and compared, we vary the number of comparative objectives from zero to four, which yields five relativeness levels.

Each task is constructed so that exactly one product satisfies every constraint and optimizes every comparative objective. We evaluate agents by the optimal-purchase rate: the fraction of tasks on which the purchased product is optimal when the full transaction is taken into account. Every environment has matched clean and steered variants with identical catalogs, instructions, and optimal purchases. The steered variant instantiates eight mechanisms drawn from documented marketplace practices (Section 3), which we also evaluate individually (Section 5), so differences between the variants isolate the effect of steering. These tasks form CAVEAT-Standard. CAVEAT-Hard keeps the same steering taxonomy and evaluation but scales each task to a catalog of 2,112 products over 88 result pages, in which exactly one product is optimal and many alternatives satisfy every constraint without being optimal. Buying the optimal product therefore requires sustained comparison across the full catalog rather than a single page of results.

We evaluate 17 model configurations spanning model families, scales, release dates, and reasoning effort, for roughly 7,000 scored trajectories. On CAVEAT-Standard, steering substantially degrades purchase quality. Under steering, the median optimal-purchase rate across configurations is 5%, and six of the 17 configurations never buy the optimal product, with failures observed in all nine environments. In contrast, the same configurations achieve a median optimal-purchase rate of 91% on matched clean tasks, indicating that the degradation stems from marketplace steering rather than task difficulty; for example, GPT-4.1 drops from 69% to 0%. Robustness improves with reasoning effort but remains limited. GPT-5.6-Sol at high reasoning effort is the only configuration to exceed 80% under steering, while the same model falls to 67% and 56% at medium and low effort despite maintaining roughly 92% on clean tasks. Performance also declines as tasks require more comparative reasoning. CAVEAT-Hard remains unsolved: even the strongest configuration fails to buy the optimal product in all ten runs, and in every run it commits to a purchase after examining only the first result page.

Trajectory analysis attributes these failures to a small set of recurring behaviors: agents restrict comparison to prominent results, settle for the first acceptable option, treat missing information as favorable, impose fixed priorities on co-equal objectives, and overlook fees or add-ons introduced at checkout. These behaviors reflect procedural shortcomings rather than a lack of capability. In a diagnostic run that forces enumeration of the full catalog, the same model that fails CAVEAT-Hard resolves all 2,112 products and identifies the optimal one within 16 steps. We therefore augment the open-source browser-use harness \citep{browseruse2024} with a domain-neutral deliberative layer. The layer compiles user instructions into explicit constraints and comparative objectives, and inserts a checkpoint before purchase that requires the agent to account for all candidate products and justify its final choice against the stated criteria. The layer uses no benchmark-specific metadata and applies unchanged across all environments. It raises GPT-5.6-Terra at low reasoning effort from 7% to 67% on steered CAVEAT-Standard tasks and GPT-5.6-Sol at high reasoning effort from 0% to 80% on CAVEAT-Hard. A prompt-only version of the same guidance reaches only 30% and 10%, respectively, so most of the gain comes from enforced deliberation rather than from the added instructions. Even with the layer, the strongest configuration still misses two of ten CAVEAT-Hard runs.

We make four contributions.

1. We formulate naturally adversarial marketplaces as a benchmark setting for CUAs and organize common marketplace influence mechanisms into an eight-type steering taxonomy spanning both deceptive dark patterns and truthful merchandising.
2. We introduce CAVEAT, a benchmark of nine controllable marketplace environments with generated catalogs, verifiable optimal purchases, matched clean and steered variants, and two difficulty tiers, CAVEAT-Standard and CAVEAT-Hard.
3. We evaluate 17 model configurations over roughly 7,000 trajectories and show that marketplace steering, rather than task difficulty, drives large performance degradations, that failures increase with the amount of comparative reasoning a task requires, that robustness improves with reasoning effort, and that even the strongest configuration fails CAVEAT-Hard.
4. We translate the observed failure modes into a domain-neutral deliberative layer for browser-use agents that yields substantial gains beyond prompt-only guidance, and we release the benchmark environments, generation pipeline, and evaluation harness.
