# Evidence for model failure and harness success

## 1. Ablations and analyses that explain why models fail

- Overall effect of steering across the benchmark. Target: Test whether the performance loss extends beyond Amazon. Implementation: We pooled all measured non-fully-absolute runs in the completed clean-versus-steered analyses covering the nine benchmark environments: 1,677 Amazon runs and 960 runs from the other eight environments, for 2,637 runs in total. Within each source dataset, the same model and task settings were evaluated in both conditions. Conclusion: Optimal-product selection fell from 1,082/1,319 (82.0%) in clean runs to 267/1,318 (20.3%) in steered runs. The effect is therefore broad across the benchmark, not specific to Amazon.

- Where the failures occur. Target: Determine whether steering mainly makes agents give up, break a hard requirement, or choose a valid but suboptimal product. Implementation: We classified the outcomes of the 1,677 Standard Amazon runs in the comparison above. Conclusion: Optimal purchases fell from 721/839 (85.9%) in clean runs to 165/838 (19.7%) in steered runs. Hard-requirement violations rose from 21 to 188, while no-purchase outcomes barely changed, from 25 to 23. Steering mainly changed what agents bought rather than whether they completed the task.

- Thematic analysis of baseline behavior. Target: Identify recurring behaviors that could explain the performance gap without using the outcome as a cue. Implementation: We conducted an outcome-hidden thematic analysis of 96 trajectories: 64 from Amazon and 32 from the other environments. The analysis used three coding passes followed by a fourth pass to resolve disagreements and combine related themes. Conclusion: The main recurring problems were narrowing the comparison around prominent products, stopping when an acceptable product appeared, losing or reordering parts of the instruction, and treating missing facts as if they had been resolved. These themes guided the targeted experiments below.

- Effects of individual and combined steering mechanisms. Target: Determine whether one mechanism dominates or several modest effects build on one another. Implementation: On the Standard laptop task, GPT-5.6-Sol-low ran eight times per condition. Optimal-product counts were 8/8 with no steering, 7/8 with pinning alone, 7/8 with burying alone, and 6/8 with both. Starting from that pinned-and-buried layout, sponsored, ranking, promotion, trust, and scarcity cues produced 5/8, 6/8, 4/8, 5/8, and 4/8. The full steering stack produced 2/8, compared with 6/8 for the matched deep-placement control, and lowered mean preference score by 0.4409. Conclusion: Individual mechanisms had modest and noisy effects in this small Standard catalog, but their combined effect was much larger.

- Capture by promoted products. Target: Test whether promotion merely changes what the agent opens first or also captures its final choice. Implementation: On the Standard laptop task, Terra-low ran eight times each under clean, sponsored, and platform-ranking conditions. We recorded product-page visits and final purchases. Conclusion: The agent bought the promoted decoy in 8/8 sponsored runs and 8/8 ranking runs, compared with 0/8 clean runs. Opening the promoted product before the final choice changed only from 6/8 clean to 8/8 steered. Promotion therefore affected the final selection, not just the first click. Because these conditions also included pinning and burying, this experiment measures the combined promoted placement rather than the label alone.

- Pinning and burying at Hard-tier scale. Target: Determine why placement steering becomes much stronger in the 2,112-product Hard catalog. Implementation: We contrasted the small effects of isolated placement in the Standard laptop task with all 10 GPT-5.6-Sol-high Hard baseline trajectories across the five product types. In Hard, the optimal product was buried in an 88-page catalog while plausible low-scoring alternatives were pinned or promoted near the front. Conclusion: All 10 runs stopped on page 1 and bought a valid but suboptimal product. At this scale, burying kept the optimal product outside the agent's comparison, while pinned and promoted alternatives supplied convincing stopping points. Hard-tier difficulty is the interaction between catalog scale and these placement steerings, not scale alone.

- Search breadth in strong-model successes and failures. Target: Find which observable search behavior separates good and bad decisions by a strong model. Implementation: We compared 112 successful and 6 unsuccessful GPT-5.6-Sol-high runs in Standard. Conclusion: Successful runs named more candidates (median 20 versus 11.5), opened more candidate product pages (median 18 versus 11.5), and used bulk information gathering more often (103/112 versus 3/6). Both groups claimed they had searched enough. Actual breadth and evidence gathering, rather than a verbal claim of completeness, separated the outcomes.

- Order of equal-priority preferences. Target: Test whether the model silently favors whichever preference is mentioned first. Implementation: In 25 matched Hard pairs, we reversed two explicitly equal-priority preferences without changing their meaning. Conclusion: The selected product changed in all 25 pairs and shifted strongly toward the preference moved to first position, even though the overall optimal-product rate barely changed. The agent was not preserving the user's stated equality between the preferences.

The evidence above identifies three main failure modes:

- Failure mode 1: Prematurely closing the candidate set around visible, promoted, or merely acceptable options.

- Failure mode 2: Losing, weakening, or inventing priorities among the user's requirements and preferences.

- Failure mode 3: Treating missing, incomplete, or misread product facts as sufficient evidence for a decision.

## 2. Changes the improved harness makes to browser-use

The improved harness keeps browser-use's ordinary browsing and checkout controls. Both the baseline and improved arms also receive the same lossless storage for very large browser results, so old output truncation cannot explain the treatment difference. The improved arm adds one general structured decision checkpoint; it receives no benchmark answers, optimal-product identities, catalog schema, or private storefront access.

- Failure mode: The agent stops after comparing a small visible subset. Implementation: Before a best-product decision can be accepted, the checkpoint requires evidence that all reachable result pages were exhausted, advertised page or item totals were reconciled, every discovered product was accounted for, and no unexamined product could still be best.

- Failure mode: The instruction drifts or equal preferences acquire an invented order. Implementation: Before browsing, the same evaluated model converts the user's instruction into a stable record that separates hard requirements from comparative preferences and preserves only priorities the user actually stated. At decision time, the checkpoint rejects products that break a hard requirement and compares the remaining products across every stated preference without using mention order as importance.

- Failure mode: Missing or conflicting facts are silently treated as favorable. Implementation: The agent submits a structured candidate record in which each needed fact is known, unknown, or conflicting. A candidate cannot pass a requirement or win while an unresolved fact could change the result. Before purchase, the agent must also recheck the visible product and basket against the approved choice and the original instruction.

## 3. Ablations and analyses that explain why the harness succeeds

- Whole-harness evaluation. Target: Test whether the complete harness improves decisions when browser-output handling is held equal. Implementation: We ran 60 evaluations forming 30 baseline-versus-harness pairs. The main comparisons contained 15 Terra-low Standard steered pairs and 10 GPT-5.6-Sol-high Hard steered pairs; both arms used the same lossless handling for large browser results. Conclusion: Optimal-product purchases rose from 1/15 to 10/15 for Terra-low and from 0/10 to 8/10 for Sol-high. The gain came from the decision process added by the harness, not from different browser-result handling.

- Prompting alone. Target: Test whether a simple request to be more careful can replace the structured harness. Implementation: On matched Hard tasks, we compared 10 baseline runs, 10 runs with a general deliberation prompt, 10 runs with the structured harness but no full-catalog requirement, and 10 runs with the full harness. Conclusion: Optimal-product counts were 0/10, 3/10, 0/10, and 10/10. Prompting helped occasionally, but the reliable improvement required the structured full-catalog check.

- Safeguards without complete search. Target: Test whether preserving the instruction, checking facts and hard requirements, and comparing local candidates are sufficient by themselves. Implementation: On the Hard laptop task, we ran eight repetitions after successively adding a general prompt, literal instruction restatement, fact and requirement checks, and exact comparison within the locally found set. Conclusion: Optimal-product counts were 0/8 for baseline, then 1/8, 1/8, 1/8, and 0/8. The local-comparison version worked harder but still searched only one results page. Better reasoning over an incomplete set did not find the global optimum.

- Full-catalog requirement in isolation. Target: Measure the effect of preventing premature stopping while holding the other harness parts fixed. Implementation: In 20 matched Hard runs, both arms used the same instruction record, fact checks, hard-requirement checks, local comparison, browser, and output handling. Only one arm also had to exhaust all result pages, reconcile the advertised totals, account for every product, and resolve every product that could still win. Conclusion: Optimal-product purchases rose from 0/10 to 10/10, and mean preference score rose from 0.149 to 1.000. This is the strongest direct evidence that preventing premature closure is the main source of the improvement.

- Requesting complete search versus blocking an incomplete decision. Target: Test whether the coverage rule must be enforced as a hard block. Implementation: On the Hard laptop task, we ran eight repetitions each with only the complete-search requirement, with local comparison plus a non-blocking complete-search request, with the full blocking harness, and with only a general prompt. Conclusion: Optimal-product counts were 8/8, 7/8, 7/8, and 1/8. A clear complete-search requirement was enough to change behavior in this sample; making the checkpoint blocking did not add a measurable gain.

- How the harness handles 2,112 products. Target: Verify that it improves search discipline rather than secretly supplying the answer. Implementation: We inspected successful Hard trajectories and compared process measures across the 30 paired runs. Successful runs accounted for all 2,112 product identities across all 88 pages using ordinary browser requests that gathered many public product pages at once. Conclusion: They finished in 19–37 model steps because one model decision could gather many products. Harness runs recorded 10.5 more candidate identities and 6.7 more product-page candidates on average. The harness supplied a stopping rule, not catalog contents or the winning identity.

- Resistance to preference-order bias. Target: Test whether the harness preserves equal preference weights. Implementation: We repeated the equal-preference order reversal under both baseline and full-harness conditions on the Hard laptop task. Conclusion: Baseline choices shifted by 0.1845 toward the preference mentioned first; the harness reduced that shift to 0.0017. Comparing all feasible candidates against all stated preferences almost eliminated the order effect.

- Remaining errors after the harness. Target: Identify what the harness still does not solve. Implementation: We reviewed successful full-catalog searches, the remaining full-harness selection failure, and harness runs that ended without a purchase. Conclusion: The harness reliably repaired early stopping, but it did not guarantee correct reading of every specification or reliable checkout. The remaining selection failure covered the full catalog but misread a specification written under an alternate label. The improvement is therefore substantial but specific: it fixes decision discipline, not every perception or action error.

## 4. Training the harness behavior into the model

- What we want the model to learn. Target: Move the harness's useful decision process into the model so it can work without the harness at inference time. Implementation: We train the model to preserve the instruction, keep track of the full option set, leave missing facts unresolved, compare all qualifying options across every stated preference, and recheck the final choice. Conclusion: The target is a general shopping policy, not memorization of benchmark products, optimal answers, or Amazon-specific fields.

- Student and teacher. Target: Isolate the value of the harness rather than distilling a more capable model. Implementation: The student and teacher both start from the same frozen Qwen3.6-27B checkpoint. The student receives ordinary browser-use history. The teacher receives that same history plus a structured summary of the harness phases, built only from storefront evidence already visible by that point in the run. Conclusion: The teacher is better organized, not more knowledgeable; it receives no optimal-product identity, score, hidden catalog data, future observation, or private storefront access.

- Training tasks. Target: Teach the general behavior without leaking Amazon evaluation examples. Implementation: We procedurally generate truthful shopping tasks from product and schema families that do not overlap Amazon's five categories. The training pool mixes clean and steered storefronts, small and large catalogs, one to four comparative preferences, reordered equal-preference instructions, satisficing requests, and cases where no product qualifies. Training, validation, and procedural-test families are kept separate. Conclusion: The model must learn when to search broadly, how to balance preferences, and when not to buy, rather than learning a fixed rule such as always enumerate or always purchase.

- Supervised warm start. Target: Give the student a reliable starting set of search, bookkeeping, tool-use, and stopping actions. Implementation: We first fine-tune a small LoRA adapter on successful baseline decisions and a bounded number of corrective next actions from the scaffolded teacher. For each corrective example, only the teacher's resulting assistant decision is used as the label; the teacher-only structured summary is removed from the student's input. Conclusion: This stage teaches the basic behavior efficiently, but by itself it mainly covers states already present in the collected demonstrations.

- On-policy context distillation. Target: Teach recovery and decision behavior at the states the changing student actually reaches. Implementation: The current student attempts fresh tasks using ordinary browser-use context. The frozen scaffolded teacher then scores the student's exact sampled actions and supplies bounded corrective actions when an important behavior is unlikely to be sampled. We update the student toward actions the teacher favors, add a small corrective and rehearsal pass, merge the adapter, and collect a fresh set of student trajectories for the next round. Conclusion: Repeating this process addresses the problem that one early browser mistake changes every later state; the training follows the student's own errors instead of relying only on a fixed offline dataset.

- Preventing shortcuts and capability loss. Target: Make sure improvement reflects the intended decision process rather than leakage, over-searching, or damaged browser skills. Implementation: Teacher state is checked to ensure that every field comes from past public observations, while Amazon tasks and outcomes are excluded from training and checkpoint selection. Clean successes and normal stopping behavior are rehearsed, and candidate checkpoints are checked for tool validity, transaction quality, clean-task performance, and excessive slowdown. Conclusion: These controls encourage the model to internalize the harness's discipline while preserving its general browser-use ability.

- Checkpoint selection. Target: Choose a trained checkpoint without tuning on the Amazon test set. Implementation: Base and trained candidates are compared on a sealed 120-task procedural panel from held-out product and schema families. A checkpoint must improve optimal-product selection by the predeclared margin while passing the safety, clean-performance, transaction, tool-validity, and runtime checks. Conclusion: Procedural validation is a leakage-safe selection screen, but it is not itself evidence of improvement on Amazon.

- Final transfer test. Target: Determine whether the behavior has actually moved into the model weights. Implementation: After checkpoint selection is frozen, the trained model and original Qwen3.6-27B model are evaluated on Amazon's five products with exactly the same unchanged baseline browser-use harness, sampling settings, and nonbinding safety limits. Neither arm receives the deliberative prompt, structured phase state, or decision checkpoint. The planned matrix covers four non-fully-absolute preference levels, clean and steered conditions, and three repetitions, for 120 runs per arm. Conclusion: Only an improvement in this matched Amazon comparison shows that the model itself learned the essence of the harness.
