# Commercial Steering in Online Marketplaces: A Taxonomy

This taxonomy defines **commercial steering** as marketplace-visible design, ranking, pricing, information, or transaction mechanisms that shift a shopper’s attention, consideration set, perceived value, trust, or final purchase path toward outcomes favored by a platform or seller, potentially against the user’s expressed preferences. The scope is limited to mechanisms already faced by human shoppers, not agent-specific attacks such as prompt injection. The taxonomy is grounded in prior work on online choice architecture, dark commercial patterns, ranking transparency, pricing salience, reviews, and marketplace interface design, especially the UK CMA’s evidence review of online choice architecture, the FTC’s work on dark patterns and deceptive pricing, the OECD’s work on dark commercial patterns, and empirical work by Mathur et al. on shopping-site dark patterns. Key sources include: [CMA online choice architecture evidence review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm), [FTC Bringing Dark Patterns to Light](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light), [OECD Dark Commercial Patterns](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/dark-commercial-patterns_44f5e846-en.html), and [Mathur et al., Dark Patterns at Scale](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns/).

## 1. Sponsored visibility and paid placement

**Sponsored visibility and paid placement** refers to commercial payment or advertising relationships that alter which products appear, where they appear, or how prominently they are displayed, while the result may still resemble ordinary marketplace content. Common examples include sponsored listings, promoted products, native ads, affiliate-driven “best” lists, and comparison rankings influenced by compensation. This category is justified by FTC concerns about ads disguised as independent content, the EU platform-to-business ranking transparency framework’s focus on whether remuneration affects ranking, and marketplace research on sponsored product listings. For LLM web agents, the core risk is that the agent treats paid prominence as evidence of relevance, quality, or user fit rather than as a commercial exposure mechanism. References: [FTC dark patterns report](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light), [EU ranking transparency guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/ranking-transparency-guidelines-framework-eu-regulation-platform-business-relations-explainer), [FTC native advertising guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/native-advertising-guide-businesses).

## 2. Platform ranking, recommendation, and self-preferencing

**Platform ranking, recommendation, and self-preferencing** refers to marketplace-controlled ordering, default sorting, recommendation modules, buy-box logic, or curated choice sets that determine what users inspect first, including cases where the platform favors its own products or preferred partners. Examples include “recommended,” “featured,” “best match,” buy-box winners, default sort orders, private-label promotion, and platform-selected badges. The category is supported by the CMA’s treatment of ranking as a core online choice architecture practice and by regulatory attention to ranking parameters and self-preferencing. For LLM web agents, the risk is over-trusting the platform’s default ranking objective instead of independently evaluating the user’s constraints, such as total cost, quality, delivery, seller reliability, or sustainability. References: [CMA OCA evidence review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm), [EU ranking transparency guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/ranking-transparency-guidelines-framework-eu-regulation-platform-business-relations-explainer).

## 3. Price obfuscation and drip or partitioned pricing

**Price obfuscation and drip or partitioned pricing** refers to presenting only part of the total price early in the shopping process, delaying mandatory fees, separating unavoidable charges, or otherwise making all-in price comparison difficult. Examples include service fees revealed at checkout, shipping or handling charges shown late, resort-style fees, “from” prices, installment framing without clear total cost, and partitioned prices that split the base price from required extras. This category is justified by the CMA’s inclusion of drip and partitioned pricing in online choice architecture, FTC work on hidden fees, and experimental evidence that drip pricing can reduce price transparency and increase spending. For LLM web agents, the main risk is optimizing for the visible base price rather than the final payable cost under the user’s actual scenario. References: [CMA OCA evidence review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm), [FTC unfair or deceptive fees rule announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/05/ftc-rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-take-effect-may-12-2025), [Blake et al., Marketing Science, 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v40y2021i4p619-636.html).

## 4. Promotional framing and reference-price manipulation

**Promotional framing and reference-price manipulation** refers to shaping perceived value through anchors, discounts, strikethrough prices, coupon salience, “was/now” comparisons, deal labels, or visual emphasis that can exaggerate savings or make a product appear better-value than it is. Examples include inflated former prices, misleading MSRP comparisons, “limited-time deal” tags, large discount percentages, and salient coupon badges. This category is grounded in the CMA’s discussion of reference pricing and framing, as well as the FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, which address former-price comparisons and comparable-value claims. For LLM web agents, the risk is accepting the marketplace’s value frame as real utility, instead of verifying whether the product actually satisfies the user’s preferences relative to alternatives and total cost. References: [CMA OCA evidence review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm), [FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, 16 CFR Part 233](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233).

## 5. Add-on defaults, bundling, and forced or sneaked outcomes

**Add-on defaults, bundling, and forced or sneaked outcomes** refers to expanding or altering the transaction through preselected options, bundles, subscriptions, warranties, upgraded variants, or required steps that add costs, commitments, products, services, or data-sharing beyond the user’s original intent. Examples include prechecked insurance, default warranty add-ons, paid delivery upgrades, “subscribe and save” defaults, automatic membership enrollment, forced account creation, and items added to the cart without clear consent. This category is supported by the CMA’s treatment of defaults, bundling, and forced outcomes, the FTC’s dark-pattern work on prechecked boxes and recurring subscriptions, and Mathur et al.’s empirical categories such as sneaking and forced action. For LLM web agents, the risk is completing a transaction whose final basket violates explicit preferences such as “no subscription,” “one-time purchase,” “no warranty,” or “under budget.” References: [CMA OCA evidence review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm), [FTC dark patterns report](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light), [Mathur et al., Dark Patterns at Scale](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns/).

## 6. Scarcity, urgency, and social-proof pressure

**Scarcity, urgency, and social-proof pressure** refers to time, availability, or popularity cues that pressure users to act faster, search less, or deliberate less. Examples include countdown timers, “only 2 left,” “selling fast,” “20 people are viewing,” “bought 100 times today,” “deal ends soon,” and “high demand” messages. The category is grounded in the CMA’s pressure-based online choice architecture mechanisms, the FTC’s discussion of countdown timers and false urgency, and Mathur et al.’s empirical documentation of urgency, scarcity, and social-proof patterns on shopping websites. For LLM web agents, the risk is premature search termination or constraint relaxation because the page implies that delay is costly or that the crowd has already validated the product. References: [CMA OCA evidence review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm), [FTC dark patterns report](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light), [Mathur et al., Dark Patterns at Scale](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns/).

## 7. Reputation and trust-signal manipulation

**Reputation and trust-signal manipulation** refers to manipulating apparent product quality, seller reliability, or social validation through reviews, ratings, testimonials, badges, endorsements, or independent-looking signals. Examples include fake reviews, paid reviews without disclosure, insider reviews, review suppression, review hijacking, inflated ratings, fake social influence indicators, and weak or ambiguous “verified” badges. This category is justified by FTC rulemaking on fake reviews and testimonials and by dark-pattern research on social proof. For LLM web agents, the risk is relying on corrupted trust proxies when judging product quality, seller reliability, or fit with the user’s preference. References: [FTC final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), [Mathur et al., Dark Patterns at Scale](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns/).

## 8. Friction, sludge, and obstruction

**Friction, sludge, and obstruction** refers to making user-preferred actions harder than platform-preferred actions by increasing effort, complexity, confusion, time, or procedural burden. Examples include hard cancellation, hard-to-find opt-outs, buried terms, confusing comparison flows, excessive steps to decline add-ons, information overload, complex language, hidden return conditions, and asymmetric checkout paths. This category is grounded in the CMA’s discussion of sludge, complex language, and information overload, the FTC’s work on hard-to-cancel subscriptions and buried terms, and Mathur et al.’s obstruction category. For LLM web agents, the risk is following the easiest available path rather than the path that best preserves the user’s stated preferences, especially in comparison shopping, cancellation, returns, subscription avoidance, or privacy-sensitive tasks. References: [CMA OCA evidence review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm), [FTC dark patterns report](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light), [Mathur et al., Dark Patterns at Scale](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns/).
